# Methods: Data Cleaning — Sleep Paper
## Complete pipeline with all steps (Maia's prose style, updated August 2026)

Morning sleep diaries were collected as one component of a larger ecological momentary assessment (EMA) protocol. Of the 13,990 EMA submissions in the export, 2,860 were morning sleep diaries: one per participant per study day, from 237 participants over up to 15 days (median 13 diary days per participant; 86.5% of all possible participant-days). Each diary recorded, as free-text responses, the times the participant went to bed, fell asleep, woke up, and got out of bed, plus self-reported sleep onset latency (SOL), wake after sleep onset (WASO), and time in bed. To process these entries quickly, reliably, and verifiably, we built a correction pipeline of ten sequential stages. Every stage logs what it did, so each run can be audited after the fact and repeated exactly.

**Stage 1: Data Loading and Schema Validation.** The pipeline reads the raw export in either `.rds` or `.csv` form and detects the format automatically. **Schema validation** means the pipeline checks, before any processing, that every column it needs exists under an expected name: participant ID, the four timestamp fields, and the duration fields. If a required column is missing or misnamed, the run stops immediately with a message naming the problem — it never continues silently on incomplete input. Optional supplementary files (for example, a metadata table) are merged at this point. The pipeline also checks the human-review input files (the `manual_*` correction CSVs, described in Stage 5). If any are configured but missing, the pipeline asks whether to continue without them, or stops the run; a run can never quietly proceed as if human corrections had been applied when they were not.

**Stage 1.5: Field-Misentry Detection (Preprocessor).** Participants sometimes type a clock time into a field that expects a duration — for example, answering the duration question "10 minutes and 30 seconds" as "10:30". The SOL and WASO fields can legitimately contain either a clock time or a duration depending on the question wording, so the pipeline cannot simply reject one format. Instead, it scans these two fields for clock-time-shaped values sitting in duration fields, and for durations that look implausibly large or small. Each suspicious entry is flagged and set aside for human review; nothing is changed at this step.

**Stage 2: Timestamp Standardization and Interval Processing.** All free-text times and durations are rewritten into one standard format. A **silent coercion error** is what happens when a program converts text into a number without checking the result — for example, turning "10:30" into the number 1030 and continuing as if nothing had gone wrong. The pipeline guards against this: every text-to-number conversion is checked, and entries that fail are flagged for review rather than silently becoming a plausible-looking number. AM/PM mistakes are corrected with conditional logic and 12-hour adjustments, with special handling for entries near midnight (bedtime and sleep onset), where such mistakes are frequent. Duration entries (SOL, WASO, naps, exercise) pass through a fixed decision procedure: (1) discard strings that cannot be a duration, (2) repair malformed formats (a missing or misplaced colon, for instance), and (3) convert the result into total minutes. Each step records the numeric output, the correction history, and any manual-review flags.

**Stage 3: Processing and Correction of Timestamp Sequence.** Participants occasionally entered timestamps in the wrong order — for example, swapping bedtime and sleep onset. The pipeline therefore enforced the chronological sequence bed → sleep → awake → get-up with a two-step algorithm, applied only to records with all four timestamps present. Step 1 resolved cross-day and AM/PM misclassifications by subtracting 12 hours, repeatedly, whenever two adjacent timestamps were more than 12 hours apart. Step 2 corrected minor inversions (less than 3 hours apart), which usually mean the two entries were typed in swapped order, by exchanging the adjacent timestamps. Identical timestamps (e.g., bedtime = sleep time) were not treated as errors. Every automated correction carried a label recording its type, so each one can be traced and audited.

**Stage 4: Record Classification and Manual Review Generation.** After timestamp normalization, records were classified into three categories: (a) Clean (no flags), (b) Flagged Errors requiring manual review, and (c) Statistically Atypical (plausible but unusual). If, after the correction steps described above, at least one of the following errors existed, the entry was flagged for manual review: (a) timestamp sequence errors; (b) calculated difference between time in bed and time asleep was >7 hours; (c) calculated difference between time of final awakening and time out of bed was >7 hours; or (d) duration of sleep calculated based on timestamps was >24 hours. Clinically plausible but statistically atypical records were also marked for human review if at least one of the following conditions were met: (a) duration of sleep calculated based on timestamps was <3 or >15 hours; (b) calculated SOL was >3 hours; (c) calculated duration in bed after final awakening >3 hours. Two review CSV files were generated at this stage, one for each category, each carrying the full diagnostic information a reviewer needs to decide the case.

**Stage 4.75: Second-Review Consensus.** For records that had already been through an initial human review, a second-review checklist was applied to confirm the reviewers agreed on each flagged item before corrections were applied. Any disagreements between reviewers were reconciled here, and the final review decisions were locked in.

**Stage 5: Human Manual Correction and Reincorporation.** Two members of the research team independently reviewed all flagged errors (n = 75) as well as statistically atypical cases (n = 37) by comparing values against all original sleep diary responses by that participant in order to understand a given sleep period in context of the rest of the study. For flagged errors, the two reviewers reached the same judgment immediately on 48 of 75 records (64.0%) and resolved the remaining 27 (36.0%) by discussion to consensus; for atypical cases the corresponding figures were 33 of 37 (89.2%) and 4 (10.8%). Because both reviewers were members of the research team with access to the pipeline output, these figures describe co-review agreement during development and are not an independent blinded reliability estimate. The human manual corrections were reincorporated into the dataset using a partially automated script to correct values using labels and natural-language processing, with checks to ensure reversibility and post-correction validation to flag discrepancies (>6 minutes) between derived intervals and reported durations. Corrections were ultimately applied to 85 of the 112 reviewed records (the remaining 27 were confirmed to need no change after review); in the final dataset these 85 records break down as 32 clean, 38 equal-time, 12 unusual, and 3 error after correction. A controlled re-run (2026-09-29) with and without the manual-correction files quantified their effect: without them, 1,719 records carried a computable total sleep time and 61 records remained in the error class; with them, 1,729 records were valid (+10 net) and only 3 remained errors. The net gain came from 11 error-classified records whose corrected timestamps made total sleep time computable for the first time (unlocked totals of 293–590 minutes), while one extreme record (TST = 10 minutes) was correctly retired by its correction.

Where a self-reported duration and the timestamp-derived interval disagreed but both values were individually plausible, the record was flagged rather than adjusted. Because this study concerns the association between sleep and same-day affect, between- and within-person variability in reported sleep timing is the signal of interest, and normalizing such disagreements toward internal consistency would risk removing it. Only records confirmed to contain a data-entry error were corrected.

**Stage 5.5: Duration-Based Corrections and Metric Acceptances.** Following human manual correction application, three secondary correction substeps were applied in sequence: (1) nap and exercise duration corrections, standardizing these allied metrics alongside sleep metrics; (2) sleep-metric duration-based corrections, addressing any remaining internal inconsistencies in derived sleep durations; and (3) human metric review acceptances, applying any manual decisions about accepting or rejecting specific computed sleep values (TST, SOL, WASO, sleep efficiency) that had been flagged during review.

**Stage 6: Derivation of Final Sleep Metrics.** The four headline metrics were computed only after all corrections were applied: sleep efficiency percentage (SE), sleep onset latency in hours (SOL_h), wake after sleep onset in hours (WASO_h), and total sleep time in hours (TST_h). Each was computed from the corrected timestamps and durations using documented formulas. As a final check, the corrected times, the self-reported durations, and the derived metrics had to agree with each other to within ±6 minutes; larger disagreements were flagged rather than silently accepted.

**Stage 7: Auto-Detection of Remaining Issues.** A post-correction classification layer evaluated all records for remaining data-quality concerns using four flag categories: TIMESTAMP (sequence or range violations), DURATION (implausible duration values), AMOUNT (missing or extreme amount-scale entries), and SELF_REPORTED (plausibility of self-report consistency). Records could carry multiple flag types, and all flags were recorded in the final flag ledger for transparency and reproducibility.

**Stage 7.5: Post-hoc Audit of Auto-Detected Candidates.** To verify that the auto-detection layer had not missed records needing correction — and had not mis-corrected any — we audited the candidates the algorithm itself surfaced as highest-confidence. The detector flagged 23 records whose adjacent timestamps were separated by 3–6 hours in the raw data (the signature of an AM/PM reversal). Two members of the research team independently reviewed all 23 candidates against the participant's full set of original diary responses; judgments were unanimous (23/23). For every candidate, the pipeline's automated correction (a 12-hour AM/PM flip, `sleep_reduce_12h_loop`) was confirmed correct against the original diary context, and no candidate required any additional manual change. An extended blind-spot screen covering 32 additional lower-confidence candidates produced the same result: all were already correctly handled by the pipeline. Because the audit found nothing to change, the manual correction files were left unchanged (75 error records and 37 atypical records, as described in Stage 5); the audit therefore serves as evidence that the automated detection and correction layer did not under- or over-correct in the real dataset.

**External Validation on an Independent Real-World Dataset.** To test whether the pipeline generalizes beyond our own data, we ran it end-to-end on a completely independent, publicly released sleep-diary dataset: the in-situ wearable dataset of Baigutanova et al. (2025, *Scientific Data*, DOI 10.1038/s41597-025-05801-3; data on Figshare, DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.28509740, CC BY), comprising daily sleep diaries from 49 healthy adults (mean age 28.4, 51% female) recorded over four weeks (1,372 diary entries) as part of a continuous smartwatch HRV monitoring study. The dataset's structure differs from ours in several ways — 24-hour clock times rather than 12-hour AM/PM entries, no separate get-up field (the wake-up time was carried into the get-up field, making wake-up == get-up for every record), and no substance-use or exercise fields (these were supplied as NA) — so the run also exercised the pipeline's column-mapping and schema-validation paths on data it was not developed against. The pipeline completed all ten stages without intervention. Of the 1,372 entries, 1,094 (79.7%) yielded computable total sleep time; mean TST was 7.55 hours, squarely within the 6–8.5 h range expected of healthy adults and matching the values the source study reports from the same diaries. No record was classified as a temporal error or as statistically atypical, and no automated 12-hour corrections were applied — consistent with the source study's statement that AM/PM confusions had been manually corrected before release. The 100% equal-time classification (wake-up reported as get-up) is the expected, benign consequence of the get-up adaptation rather than a pipeline artifact. Two defects in the pipeline's input handling were exposed and fixed during this exercise (a preprocessing step that assumed an RDS-format main input, crashing with a cryptic error on CSV inputs; and an interval processor that skipped contract-column generation for all-NA fields, crashing finalization on datasets lacking optional fields), which independently confirms the value of external validation as a test surface.

**Stage 8: Cross-Participant Consistency Check.** A global consistency audit compared individual records against group-level distributions (mean, SD, outlier thresholds) to detect anomalies such as one participant's systematic sleep duration being incompatible with the measurement instrument or study design. This stage produced summary reports flagging systematic inconsistencies but did not alter individual records; any action on such flags required manual review.

**Stage 9: Diagnostic Figure Generation and QC Output.** Automated generation of 28 diagnostic and research-oriented figures (12 quality-control plots in `pipeline_cleaning/`, 16 research analyses in `research_ready/`), a figure index contact sheet, and a run-info summary document. These figures provide visual confirmation of data quality and distribution characteristics, enabling rapid human inspection of pipeline output quality; each figure is documented with its exact rules, thresholds, and expected-vs-anomalous appearance in the package vignette ("Interpreting the Pipeline Output", Sections 6–7).

**Stage 10: Dataset Finalization.** Final columns were selected, renamed, and ordered according to a pre-specified column dictionary, producing two deliverable datasets: Dataset A (complete, with all intermediate columns kept so every change can be audited) and Dataset B (minimal, public-ready subset for analysis and archiving). In the delivered real-data run, 1,729 of the 13,990 exported entries (12.4%) carried computed sleep metrics for analysis.

### New stages added in v1.4.4

- **Stage 1.5**: Field-misentry preprocessor (SOL/WASO clock-time vs duration confusion detection)

> **Field-misentry silent-misrepair fix (v1.4.4+).** During pipeline development we discovered a silent-misrepair failure mode affecting the SOL (sleep-onset latency) and WASO (wake-after-sleep-onset) fields. Participants occasionally enter a clock time (e.g., "11:30") into a field that expects a duration in minutes. Before v1.4.4 the pipeline could silently reinterpret that clock time as a plausible-looking duration and write a wrong value into the cleaned dataset without any flag — the worst failure mode for a data-cleaning pipeline (wrong and silent, rather than wrong and visible).

An initial synthetic benchmark (2026-08-12, pre-patch) injected 400 field-misentry errors into each of the SOL and WASO fields and ran the then-current pipeline. The results showed pervasive silent misrepair: **field_misentry_sol** was silently misrepaired in 383/400 cases (95.8%); **field_misentry_waso** in 384/400 (96.0%). Only 4.2% (SOL) and 3.0% (WASO) were caught or corrected at all — the worst failure mode for a data-cleaning pipeline.

The fix (commit `5dd0e27`, 2026-08-12, Part A4 in `checkforerrors_processing.R`) added a targeted check that flags suspicious clock-time-shaped duration entries for human review instead of silently accepting a reinterpreted value. However, the synthetic benchmark result files committed to the repository one hour after the patch (commit `18c3ed1`) were the *pre-patch* run that originally discovered the bug — not a post-patch re-run. A "Patch verification" table in the project documentation cited post-patch numbers (SOL 95.8% → 3.5% misrepaired; WASO 95.8% → 0%) but those figures were hand-derived from an ad-hoc check, not from a regenerated benchmark CSV; no post-patch CSV existed in the repository.

We re-ran the full benchmark end-to-end on 2026-09-02 against the currently installed `sleepcleanr` 1.4.5 (Part A4 patch included) — full `corrupted_enrichment.rds` (n=7,000, all 15 error categories together, participant-clustered injection), evaluated with `evaluate_detection.R` (v4) against the current `ground_truth_enrichment.csv`. This produced the first real post-patch numbers for this finding:

| | field_misentry_sol | field_misentry_waso |
|---|---|---|
| **CORRECT (auto-fixed to the true value)** | 7/400 (1.8%) | 13/400 (3.2%) |
| **FLAGGED_UNRESOLVED** (caught, routed to human review) | 393/400 (98.2%) | 387/400 (96.8%) |
| **MISREPAIRED** (silently wrong) | **0/400 (0%)** | **0/400 (0%)** |

The silent-misrepair bug is **fully closed** for both fields — 0% misrepaired, actually stronger than the earlier hand-derived claim of a 3.5% residual for SOL (that residual does not reproduce on the current code; not further investigated, but noted as an open thread rather than claiming certainty about why).

**Important nuance:** the pipeline now routes virtually all field-misentry cases to human review (FLAGGED_UNRESOLVED: 98.2% SOL, 96.8% WASO). Only 1.8% (SOL) and 3.2% (WASO) are auto-corrected to the correct value (CORRECT). The previously cited "96.5% caught" figure conflated auto-correction with human-review routing; the pipeline's real effect is converting a silent wrong answer into a **human-review flag**, not an automatic fix. Both are legitimate, safe outcomes — neither delivers a wrong value silently — but they are different claims and should be reported separately.
